# From 2,000 Bookmarks to Five Paths: A Curation Story

I didn't start with a system. I started with a browser export that had the file size of a short novel: somewhere north of 2,000 bookmarks, accumulated over roughly four years of "I should read this."

The uncomfortable part wasn't the number. It was that I could name maybe ten of them. The rest were a fossil record of past intentions.

## The moment of truth

The prompt was mundane: a friend asked how to get into web development. "Send me some good links," he said.

I went into my bookmarks to pull together a starter list and spent forty-five minutes failing. I had *plenty* — three different React intros, a CSS grid cheat sheet saved twice, something called "advanced webpack" from a job I no longer had. What I didn't have was an answer to the simplest question a learner asks: **where do I start?**

Twenty of those 2,000 links, in the right order, would have been a better gift than everything I owned. I had mistook possession for knowledge, and the export proved it.

## The audit

I did what anyone does after an unpleasant realization: I made a spreadsheet I'd abandon later. But one habit from it stuck — the three-bucket rule for every single link:

1. **Promote.** If the link answered "would I send this to a friend as *the* starting point?", it went into a path.
2. **Park.** Genuinely interesting, not currently relevant — it went into one single inbox. Not fifteen folders. One.
3. **Delete.** Everything else. Including the advanced-webpack thing. *Especially* the advanced-webpack thing.

The numbers after one weekend: ~120 links promoted, ~60 parked, and yes — about 1,800 deleted. I felt nothing for 1,790 of them. That's how I knew they were never mine to keep.

## The structure that stuck

Promoted links went into paths, and over a few months it settled into exactly five: web fundamentals, React, a Python path for scripting, one for this newsletter's topic, and one embarrassingly useful path of "how our household handles finances." Each was small — a dozen links or so — each had sections in a deliberate order.

The park inbox got emptied every Friday. Promote or delete, no third option. Fifteen minutes, weekly, no exceptions — that cadence turned out to matter more than any rule above, because it's the week where you're still *inside* the topic that you know which link deserves the seat.

## What actually changed

- **Lookups died.** "Where's that good Postgres article" became a URL I could send in ten seconds.
- **Completion went up, weirdly.** Not on paths I saved for later — on paths I'd *built*. Building a path means reading the links. Curation turned out to be the studying.
- **Sharing made me honest.** Every path I sent to someone got a fix before it went out: a dead link, a missing intro, an ordering that only made sense to me.
- **The pile shrank and stayed shrunk.** Five paths, plus an inbox I empty weekly. The 2,000-link anxiety has not come back.

## The lessons I'd keep

1. **Curation beats collection.** The skill isn't finding links; it's refusing most of them.
2. **Sequence is the value.** The same ten links in a good order teach; in a pile they don't.
3. **Teach early.** Sending a path to one person will fix more than a month of private tidying.
4. **A path is a living document.** Two of my five have been rewritten entirely since. That's not failure — that's the point.

I still save links. The difference is where they land: one inbox, emptied weekly, promoted into paths or deleted. It's less romantic than a second brain. It just works.
