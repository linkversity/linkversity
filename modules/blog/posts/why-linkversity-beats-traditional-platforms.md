# Courses vs. Curated Paths: Pick the Right Tool

Every few months someone asks whether Linkversity "replaces Coursera" or "is better than Udemy." The honest answer is: not usually, and it depends. They're different tools, and knowing which one your problem needs is worth more than any feature table.

## What course platforms are genuinely good at

Credit where it's due. A good course platform gives you:

- **A finished curriculum.** Someone sequenced the material, tested it on strangers, and fixed the worst gaps. That's real work and it shows.
- **Accountability machinery.** Deadlines, progress bars, cohorts, certificates. Clunky, sometimes gamified to the point of insult — but structure gets people over the finish line that pure willpower doesn't.
- **Production value.** When the difference between understanding and not is one good diagram, professionally made content earns its price.
- **Credentials.** For career switches and hiring signals, a certificate from a recognized program still opens doors a link collection cannot.

If you're entering a field cold, want structure, or need the signal — take the course. This isn't false modesty; a curated path of twelve links will not teach you machine learning.

## Where courses break down

The trouble starts when your learning problem is *specific*:

- **The content moved on.** Course production cycles run 6–18 months. In fast-moving areas — a new framework version, a security practice, an API that changed last Tuesday — the best material is a blog post from six weeks ago that no course has absorbed yet.
- **The context is yours.** No course teaches *your* codebase, *your* deployment process, *your* team's conventions. This is exactly what onboarding and internal training are made of, and no platform will ever sell it to you.
- **The map is scattered.** Sometimes the right learning is three docs pages, one conference talk, a sticky-thread discussion, and the project's own README. The value is in the *selection and order* — which is precisely what a course can't pre-package for your case.
- **You already know what you need.** Experienced learners often need four specific things, not a 40-hour program. Forcing a course-shaped solution onto a path-shaped problem wastes weekends.

## The honest comparison

| | Course platform | Curated path |
|---|---|---|
| Source of material | One producer, produced content | Anything — docs, videos, posts, repos |
| Freshness | Months to years behind | Updated the day reality changes |
| Context fit | Generic by necessity | Yours, by definition |
| Accountability | Built in | Comes from the audience you share with |
| Cost | $50–500+, or subscription | Free (in Linkversity, genuinely) |
| Best for | New fields, structured ramps, credentials | Scattered material, fast-moving topics, teams |

## The best learners quietly do both

Watch how strong engineers actually learn: a course to enter a field, then — the moment the course ends — a personal path of the docs, talks, and posts they're *really* using, constantly pruned. The course gets you in the door; the path is how you stay current.

The two aren't rivals. A course is a *finished artifact*; a path is a *living one*. Problems that stay still get courses. Problems that move get paths.

## Where Linkversity sits

We're firmly, unsurprisingly, in the path camp: bring links from anywhere, order them into sections, keep them current, share the URL with whoever needs it — teammates, students, the internet. It's the living half of the picture, the part no course platform can do because it's made of the thing only you have: what *your* learning actually looks like this month.

So: use the course. Then build the path that keeps it alive.
